Water Heater Expansion Tank in Camarillo, CA

.

Water expands when it heats, and in a closed plumbing system it has nowhere to go. An expansion tank gives it somewhere. This guide explains how the tank works, why Camarillo homes often have a closed system, how to spot trouble and what installation involves.

What thermal expansion does to a house

Cold water becomes hot water in your heater, and as it heats, its volume rises by a small but real amount. In an open system, the extra water pushes back toward the street through the service line. In a closed system, a check valve or a pressure reducing valve blocks that path, so the extra volume goes nowhere and pressure climbs inside your pipes and heater.

That pressure spike happens every time the heater runs. Over months it can weep from the temperature and pressure relief valve, shorten the life of the tank, and wear out fixtures and washing machine valves. An expansion tank is a small pressurized vessel on the cold supply near the heater. A rubber diaphragm inside it separates air from water. When the water expands, the diaphragm moves and absorbs the surge.

Why many Camarillo homes have a closed system

A closed system forms when a backflow preventer, a check valve or a pressure reducing valve sits on the incoming line. Many homes have one or more of these, especially where the street pressure is high and a regulator was added.

Camarillo grew from about 2,400 people in 1960 to over 52,000 in 1990, so homes from several building eras share the same streets, and the equipment ahead of the heater varies from house to house.

You cannot tell by looking at the street. The test is on the pipe. If you find a pressure reducing valve where the main line enters the house, your system is probably closed. An expansion tank is then a reasonable part of any water heater installation. If you are replacing a heater, ask whether a tank is needed. Signs the system needs an expansion tank

These signs can have other causes, so measure the pressure first.

Dripping from the relief valve

A valve that weeps each time the heater runs often points to pressure spikes.

Banging or thumping pipes after the heater cycles

Pressure changes shake pipes and fittings.

Pressure above normal on a hose bib gauge

A gauge reading over about 80 psi at idle is a flag. Check before you act.

Short life of toilet fill valves and washer hoses

Repeated failures hint at strain from pressure.

A tank that sat for years with no tank

Adding a tank to an older heater can calm the pressure.

How we install an expansion tank

A tank is a short job when the cold line has room.

Measure house pressure

We read the static pressure with a gauge on a hose bib before touching anything.

Match the tank charge

The tank has an air charge set at the factory. We set it to match your house pressure before it goes on the line.

Pick the spot

The tank goes on the cold supply near the heater and is supported, since it can be heavy when full.

Connect and bleed

We shut off the supply, tie in the tank with a tee, open a tap to bleed the air and check for leaks.

Verify

We watch the pressure through a heating cycle and confirm the relief valve stays dry.

Sizing and the air charge

Tanks come in different sizes, and the right one depends on the water volume in the system and the pressure. Most homes need a small unit, often 2 gallons. Larger heaters or high pressure may call for a bigger one.

The air charge is the detail that most people miss. If the charge is lower than the house pressure, the diaphragm sits flat against the water side and the tank does nothing.

You can check the charge on an installed tank with a tire gauge at the air valve, after turning off the supply and relieving the water pressure. A waterlogged tank feels heavy and is full of water at the air valve. These tanks wear out over time and are inexpensive to replace. For heaters with other problems, our water heater repair team can check the whole system.
