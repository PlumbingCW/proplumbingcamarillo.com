What the flange does

A toilet flange is a ring fixed to the floor around the drain opening. Two bolts slot into it and hold the toilet in place. A wax ring or a rubber seal sits between the toilet horn and the flange and keeps water and gas from escaping. If the flange is solid and at the right height, the seal stays tight for years.

When the flange fails, the seal does too. Water seeps out at the base with each flush, soaks the subfloor and rots the wood underneath. Because the leak is below the toilet, you may not see it until the floor feels soft or a stain shows on the ceiling below. Catching it early saves a lot of repair work.

Signs the flange is the problem

One of these is enough reason to pull the toilet and look.

A toilet that rocks or shifts

Loose bolts or a broken flange let the bowl move.

Water around the base after flushing

The wax seal is leaking, often because the flange is low or cracked.

A sewer smell near the toilet

A bad seal lets gas out of the drain.

A soft or spongy floor near the toilet

Long term leaks rot the subfloor.

Bolts that spin or will not tighten

The slots in the flange are broken or rusted through.

Flange sitting below the finished floor

After a tile remodel, the flange can end up too low for a seal to hold.

Why flanges fail in older Camarillo homes

Camarillo grew quickly in the 1960s through the 1980s, and homes from those years may have cast iron, plastic or brass flanges that are now decades old. Cast iron rusts, plastic cracks and brass can corrode where it meets the drain pipe. A remodel that adds tile or a thicker floor can leave the flange sunk below the surface.

The City of Camarillo reports well water hardness averaging 217.6 ppm, which leaves scale on bolts and fittings. Seized bolts are a common reason a toilet will not come off cleanly and a flange breaks during removal. If you have a toilet that keeps running or leaking, our toilet repair page covers the other usual faults.

How we repair a flange

A good repair starts with a clean look at what is under the toilet.

Shut off and drain

We close the supply valve, flush the tank and sponge out the remaining water.

Remove the toilet

We disconnect the supply line, remove the nuts and lift the toilet straight up.

Inspect the flange and floor

We clean off the old wax, check the flange height and probe the floor for soft spots.

Repair or replace

Options include a repair ring, a stainless steel ring, an extension ring for a low flange or full replacement.

Reset the toilet

We set a new seal, tighten the bolts evenly and test with several flushes.

Wax rings, rubber seals and the height rule

A standard wax ring works when the flange sits at or slightly above the finished floor. If the flange is below the floor, a thicker or double wax ring may help in a pinch, but it is better to raise the flange so the seal sits flat. Rubber seals are reusable and tolerate small shifts, and they are a common pick in repairs.

Whichever seal you choose, set the toilet once. Lifting it after the wax has compressed breaks the seal, and a repeat visit costs more time. We also check that the toilet is level, the bolts are snug and the caulk or shims are in the right spots. If the toilet is old and worn, see toilet replacement options, and for drains that run slow, our drain cleaning page.

Choosing and setting a toilet after the repair

A toilet that rocks is not always a flange problem, so the final check matters. Set the toilet on the new seal with the bolts only hand tight, press down evenly, then tighten the nuts a little at a time on each side.

Over tightening can crack the porcelain. Once the bowl is firm, trim the bolt tops and cap them. Run the toilet through several flushes and look for water at the base and the supply line.

If the toilet is older and the finish is worn, a replacement is a reasonable time to switch to a modern unit. Our toilet repair and installation page covers sizing, rough-in distance and seat height. A new toilet also helps with water use, because newer designs flush with less water than models from the 1970s and 1980s.

Local note: older flooring and Camarillo remodels

Camarillo had about 52,000 residents by 1990 and 70,741 by 2020, which means much of its housing was built in the decades between. Floors in many of those homes have been redone at least once, and a new tile or plank floor often leaves the toilet flange too low.
