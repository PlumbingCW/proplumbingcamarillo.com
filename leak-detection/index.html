Start with the meter: is water really leaking?

Before anyone opens a wall, a meter check answers the first question. Turn off every tap, the dishwasher, the washer and the ice maker. Look at your water meter and check whether the leak indicator, often a small triangle or dial, is still moving.

If it is, water is leaving the system somewhere. If it holds still, the leak may be on the fixture side or the problem may be a running toilet.

The next step is the main shutoff. Close the valve where the line enters the house and recheck the meter. If the meter still moves, the leak is in the line between the meter and the house, the yard or under the driveway. If it stops, the leak is inside the building. That one test splits the search in half and often saves hours.

Symptoms that point to a hidden leak

Callers often describe the symptom rather than the word leak. These are the signs we see most.

High water bill with no change in habits

Even a pinhole leak running day and night adds up over a billing cycle.

Meter moving with fixtures off

This is the clearest sign of a leak somewhere in the system.

Wet or stained ceiling or wall

A supply line, drain or fixture above is leaking. Water travels along framing, so the stain may be feet from the source.

Warm spot on the floor

A hot water line under a slab is leaking, which is the pattern covered on our slab leak repair page.

Sound of running water at night

A hiss or trickle when the house is quiet means water is moving where it should not.

Low pressure in one area

A leak ahead of that fixture or a failing line can drop pressure at the end of the run.

How we find the source without tearing out walls

We use electronic listening equipment that picks up the sound of pressurized water escaping, then a ground microphone or correlator to pinpoint the spot. Thermal imaging can show a hot supply line leak under a floor. A pressure test on isolated sections of pipe tells us which branch is losing pressure. In many cases the work ends with a marked spot on a floor or wall, which keeps demolition small.

After the source is found, you decide on the repair path. A spot repair in a wall is simple. A leak in a copper line under a slab can be repaired through the slab or avoided by rerouting overhead. The right choice depends on pipe age and condition, which we cover on our repiping and water line repair pages.

Where leaks hide in Camarillo homes

In a slab-on-grade house, supply and hot water lines often run through or under the concrete. A leak there rarely shows until the floor is warm, the baseboards swell or the bill rises. In two-story homes, the leak is usually in a wall or ceiling below a bathroom.

Other common spots include the supply line to a toilet or sink, the water heater connections, an irrigation line near the meter, a pool or spa line and the main line between meter and house. For irrigation and yard leaks, the meter test is especially useful because it separates the lines on the street side from the house plumbing.

How leak testing relates to the plumbing code

Pressure testing is the basic method plumbers use to test new piping under the California Plumbing Code (CPC), which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and the same idea works on older systems. We isolate a section, hold pressure and watch for a drop. Electronic listening and thermal tools then narrow the spot, so a repair does not mean cutting more than it must.

Slab homes, raised foundations and crawl spaces

Many Camarillo homes sit on concrete slabs, so lines run under the floor or overhead and a listening tool is the way to find a leak. A home on a raised foundation lets a plumber look at the pipes from below, which makes the search faster and the repair simpler. Tell us which kind of foundation you have when you call.

A buried line between the meter and the house is a separate search. A soggy patch, a sunken strip of lawn or a meter that moves with the house valve closed all point to it, and our water line repair page explains the fix.
